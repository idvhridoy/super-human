# Statecraft Curriculum

> Maintained by `@domain-mentor`. The full L0→L10 path; each level lists skills, theory, and the assessment that unlocks the next level.
> Framing rule: the smart ruler — study power to understand it, check it, and one day wield it responsibly. Analytical and educational throughout: simulation, history, and texts only — never real-world manipulation of people. `geopolitics/` covers how states interact externally, `history/` supplies the case material, `strategy/` owns decision frameworks and game theory, `leadership/` covers the human side — this domain synthesizes them at the level of governance. Reference, never repeat.

## L0 — Foundations: What Rulers Actually Do
- Skills: name the real jobs of anyone who governs — decide, allocate, enforce, represent, plan; watch a school/family/local decision and identify who actually holds each function.
- Theory: leadership titles hide the work; every ruler faces the same five jobs whether a village elder or a president; authority, responsibility, and accountability are three different things.
- Assessment: explain the five functions with a real observed example of each; describe one local decision end-to-end — who decided, who paid, who enforced, who benefited.

## L1 — Resources & Constraints
- Skills: read a simple budget as a statement of priorities; identify scarcity, trade-offs, and opportunity cost at group/state scale.
- Theory: every choice is a non-choice of something else; resources are finite, wants are not; budgets reveal real priorities better than speeches; `finance/` covers personal-scale money — applied here to collective resources.
- Assessment: analyze one real budget (school club, municipal summary, or simulated) — name the top 3 revealed priorities and what was traded away.

## L2 — Incentives & Rule Design
- Skills: predict how people will respond to a rule by asking "what does this reward?"; spot unintended consequences in real rules.
- Theory: people respond to incentives, not intentions; a rule's actual effect = its incentives × human behavior, not its stated goal; good rulers design for the behavior they want, not the behavior they wish for.
- Assessment: incentive autopsy — dissect 3 real rules (school, game, public policy) — stated goal vs actual incentives vs observed outcome; at least one unintended consequence identified.

## L3 — Classic Strategy I: Sun Tzu, Read Critically
- Skills: extract the core principles of *The Art of War* — know yourself and the situation, win without fighting where possible, terrain/position, timing — and apply them to a simulated conflict.
- Theory: the text is a manual for minimizing cost, not glorifying war; deception and positioning are described as effectiveness mechanics — the learner's job is to understand them analytically, not practice them on people.
- Assessment: written analysis — 5 principles explained in own words, each applied to one historical case and one safe simulated scenario.

## L4 — Classic Strategy II: Machiavelli, Read Honestly
- Skills: read *The Prince* (or excerpts) as a description of power mechanics; separate "effective" from "right" explicitly; critique the text rather than adopt it.
- Theory: Machiavelli wrote what rulers do, not what they should do — the ethics discussion is the point of the level; power acquired by cruelty has known costs and failure modes; understanding manipulation mechanics is defense and analysis, not a license.
- Assessment: critical essay — one Machiavellian recommendation analyzed three ways: does it work, what does it cost, what would a good ruler do instead.

## L5 — Diplomacy & Alliances
- Skills: map a negotiation — interests vs positions, BATNA, trust-building; explain why alliances form, hold, and break.
- Theory: states/groups cooperate when interests align and defect when they diverge — alliances are maintained, not assumed; diplomacy is the art of finding overlaps and making commitments credible; treaties are promises with enforcement problems.
- Assessment: run one simulated negotiation/alliance exercise — interests mapped, deal reached or failed with reasons; debrief names what built or broke trust.

## L6 — Institutions & Governance
- Skills: explain constitutions, courts, bureaucracies, elections, and checks-and-balances as tools that outlive rulers; compare how two systems handle the same problem.
- Theory: good institutions let ordinary people govern decently and restrain bad rulers; institutions are rules about rules; strongman rule scales badly and ends badly — the historical record is the evidence.
- Assessment: compare two governance systems on one real problem (succession, corruption, dispute resolution) — written analysis of which institutional design handles it better and why.

## L7 — Legitimacy & the Consent of the Governed
- Skills: distinguish coercion from legitimacy; trace where a real authority's right to rule comes from; recognize legitimacy-crisis patterns.
- Theory: force can compel obedience but not durable order; legitimacy sources — consent, tradition, competence, law, delivery — each has strengths and failure modes; rulers who lose legitimacy keep power only by rising coercion.
- Assessment: case analysis — one historical or current legitimacy crisis explained: which legitimacy source failed, what coercion replaced it, how it ended.

## L8 — The Long Game
- Skills: think in decades — infrastructure, education, institution-building; plan for succession and contingencies; distinguish durable wins from short-term wins.
- Theory: the best rulers plant trees whose shade they won't sit in; compounding applies to institutions and trust, not just money; every plan needs a succession plan and a "what if we're wrong" branch; `strategy/` owns scenario-planning frameworks — applied here at civilizational scale.
- Assessment: 20-year simulated plan — one domain (a simulated city/club/state) developed across shocks, with succession and contingency branches, defended before `@domain-mentor`.

## L9 — Crisis Statecraft
- Skills: govern under pressure — incomplete information, time limits, escalation/de-escalation choices; keep ethics functional when afraid.
- Theory: crises compress time and inflate stakes; the failure modes are panic, paralysis, and escalation spirals; historical crises show that calm, honest communication, and pre-built institutions decide outcomes more than brilliance.
- Assessment: timed crisis simulation — escalating scenario governed over multiple rounds; decisions logged with reasoning; debrief graded on decision quality, communication, and ethical consistency under pressure.

## L10 — Mastery: Governing a Simulated Scenario — Capstone
- Skills: govern a rich simulated domain end-to-end — budget, rules, diplomacy, institutions, crisis — over a multi-session arc.
- Theory: synthesis — statecraft is the integration of allocation, incentives, strategy, diplomacy, legitimacy, and the long game; the measure of the ruler is what the governed inherit.
- Assessment: capstone — full simulated governance run (documented decisions, reasoning, outcomes across ≥5 scenario events), final state reviewed by `@domain-mentor` on prosperity, legitimacy, and ethical consistency; learner writes a "what I learned about power" reflection.
