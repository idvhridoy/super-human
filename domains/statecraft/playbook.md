# Statecraft Playbook

> Do/don't list maintained by `@domain-mentor` from session evidence.

## Do
- Keep the framing analytical and educational — we study power to understand it, check it, and (someday) wield it responsibly for others
- Discuss the ethics of power honestly — "does it work?" and "is it right?" are always two questions, both mandatory
- Read Machiavelli, Sun Tzu, and co. critically — descriptions of what rulers do, not instructions for the learner
- Ground every concept in cases — real history and current events from `history/` and `geopolitics/` make theory legible
- Use simulations and games for practice — strategy games, written scenarios, tabletop exercises are the lab
- Trace incentives before judging outcomes — most "bad governance" is predictable behavior meeting bad rule design
- Study legitimacy, not just force — durable rule rests on consent, competence, and institutions, not coercion alone
- Ask "what would a good ruler do instead?" after every cynical passage — the counterfactual is where judgment grows
- Connect to `leadership/` — the human-scale version of the same questions; power done well looks like service
- Log decisions in simulations with reasoning — reviewing your own governance choices is half the training

## Don't
- **HARD RULE: never practice manipulation, deception, or power plays on real people** — classmates, family, friends are off-limits; simulation, history, and fiction only
- Don't romanticize strongmen or cruelty — the historical record on coercion-based rule is clear; teach it honestly
- Don't teach tactics without the ethics discussion — every "how power works" lesson pairs with "what it costs" and "what's right"
- Don't treat the learner's own ambitions as a case to manipulate others — redirect to analysis or simulation
- Don't repeat `strategy/` game theory or `geopolitics/` international-relations depth — reference them and apply at governance scale
- Don't let simulations reward pure ruthlessness without consequence — scoring includes legitimacy, welfare, and ethical consistency, not just "winning"
- Don't present any single system (democracy, monarchy, technocracy) as obviously finished — every design has failure modes worth studying
- Don't dive into live partisan politics as course material — use history and neutral cases; partisanship analysis belongs to `fact-checking/`/`critical-thinking/` methods
- Don't skip the reflection — power studied without self-examination produces cynics, not rulers
