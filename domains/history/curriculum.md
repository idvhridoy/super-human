# History Curriculum

> Maintained by `@domain-mentor`. The full L0→L10 path; each level lists skills, theory, and the assessment that unlocks the next level.
> Framing rule for every level: age-appropriate, multi-perspective. History is an argument built from evidence — we always ask "who is telling this story, and who is missing?"

## L0 — Foundations
- Skills: build a personal timeline (birth to today); place events in order; use BCE/CE and century labels.
- Theory: the past vs history vs evidence; primary vs secondary sources; why accounts of the same event differ.
- Assessment: order 10 mixed events on a timeline correctly; explain "source" and "evidence" in own words.

## L1 — Earliest Civilizations
- Skills: map ancient civilizations to rivers/regions; compare two societies on a shared checklist.
- Theory: farming revolution → surplus → cities; Mesopotamia, Egypt, Indus Valley, early China; writing and why it began.
- Assessment: one-page comparison of two civilizations (geography, food, writing, government, one lasting legacy).

## L2 — Classical World
- Skills: trace one empire's rise and fall on map + timeline.
- Theory: Greece (city-states, democracy, philosophy), Rome (republic → empire), Persian and Maurya empires, Han China; contact and trade (Silk Road).
- Assessment: storyboard or narrate how one classical society was governed, naming who had power and who did not.

## L3 — Medieval & Post-Classical World (~500–1500)
- Skills: connect events across regions on one shared timeline.
- Theory: Islamic Golden Age, medieval Europe (feudalism, Church), Tang/Song China, kingdoms of Africa (Mali, Great Zimbabwe), the Americas (Maya, Aztec, Inca); Mongol conquests and exchange.
- Assessment: pick 3 regions and explain one achievement and one challenge each — at least one non-European region required.

## L4 — Early Modern (~1450–1750)
- Skills: map Columbian exchange flows (goods, crops, people, disease) in both directions.
- Theory: printing press and its effects, Renaissance, Reformation, gunpowder empires (Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal), exploration and colonization — told from colonizer AND colonized perspectives.
- Assessment: dual-perspective account of one encounter (e.g., conquistadors vs Aztec sources): what does each side's record emphasize?

## L5 — Intermediate milestone: Junior Historian
- Skills: run a complete source analysis: origin, author, audience, purpose, reliability, what's missing.
- Theory: combining L0–L4 — timelines, causation (short- vs long-term causes), change and continuity.
- Assessment: analyze a set of 4–6 mixed primary/secondary sources on one event; produce a 2-page "what really happened" report citing every source used.

## L6 — Modern Era: Revolutions (~1750–1900)
- Skills: separate causes into long-term/short-term/trigger categories.
- Theory: Enlightenment ideas, American/French/Haitian revolutions compared, Industrial Revolution and its human costs, nationalism, 19th-century imperialism.
- Assessment: comparative essay on two revolutions — ideas, methods, outcomes, and who benefited.

## L7 — The World Wars (1900–1945)
- Skills: build a multi-cause diagram for WWI; trace escalation steps between wars.
- Theory: alliances, imperial rivalry, nationalism → WWI; treaty of Versailles, Great Depression, rise of totalitarianism → WWII; the Holocaust and civilian experience (age-appropriate framing).
- Assessment: cause-and-consequence essay explaining how WWI's settlement contributed to WWII, using at least 3 source types.

## L8 — Post-1945 World
- Skills: track decolonization cases on a map; explain one Cold War crisis from both blocs' viewpoints.
- Theory: Cold War structure (superpowers, proxy wars, nuclear deterrence), decolonization across Asia/Africa, civil rights movements, founding of the UN and modern institutions, Bangladesh's Liberation War as regional case study.
- Assessment: dual-perspective analysis of one Cold War crisis + one decolonization story told through a local voice.

## L9 — Historiography & Contested History
- Skills: detect how the same event is narrated differently across eras, nations, and political frames.
- Theory: historiography — how historians work, schools of interpretation, revisionism, memory vs history, propaganda and myth-making; evaluate textbook vs scholarly vs popular versions.
- Assessment: write a historiographical review — one event, three different tellings, explaining *why* each differs (author, era, agenda).

## L10 — Mastery: Independent Research
- Skills: design and complete an original research project end to end: question → sources → analysis → argument → citation.
- Theory: synthesis of all levels; archive/library/online-source skills; the limits of what evidence can prove.
- Assessment: capstone — a 8–10 page independent research project on a self-chosen question, with primary sources, a bibliography, and an oral defense with mentor Q&A.
